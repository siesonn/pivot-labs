Coach, share, or direct

Someone on your team comes to you with something. Maybe they’re stuck, or deciding, or about to try something new. It’s tempting to jump in with the answer. Sometimes that’s right, and sometimes a good question helps more. This guide helps you choose, starting with what they need from you right now.

Start with what they need

Go by what they’ve asked for, or what has helped before, not a guess about them.

  • Coach

    They need room to think it through and reach their own answer. You mostly ask.

  • Share

    They’ve asked how you, or others, handled something like this. You tell stories, not instructions, and they decide what fits.

  • Direct

    The work is new to them, or something needs to change soon. You’re clear about what good looks like, then ask more as they find their feet.

Not sure which? Ask them what would help most.

Seven approaches, from most asking to most telling

Most askingMost telling

When they need room to think: four ways to coach

All four move through the same parts: what they want, where things stand, options, and a next step. Each one spends most of its time on a different part.

  • Find the goal

    Help them work out what they want to grow toward, then pick one first step.

    Best when they want to grow and the goal isn’t clear yet.

  • Build on what’s working

    Look at times it went a little better, and build the next step from there.

    Best when they’re stuck on something.

  • Weigh the options

    Lay out the choices, what each one gains and costs, and what matters most to them.

    Best when they have a decision to make.

  • Turn it into action

    Agree on a first step, a date, and how they’ll know it’s done, in their words.

    Best when they know what they want to change and need to get started.

When something seems off

  • Reconnect with the work

    Say what you’ve noticed, ask what’s changed, and listen before you suggest anything.

    Best when someone doesn’t seem as engaged as usual.

When they want your experience or your direction

  • Share what you’ve learned

    Ask what they want to know, share your experience as stories rather than instructions, and let them decide what fits.

    Best when they’ve asked how you or others handled something like this.

  • Start with clear direction

    Explain what good looks like and the first steps, then shift to asking as they find their feet.

    Best when the work is new to them or something needs to change soon.

An example

Example: three weeks with Maya

The same person can need something different from one week to the next.

  • Week 1: Build on what’s working

    Maya says the Acme proposal is stuck.

    “When has this gone even a little better? What was different then?”

  • Week 2: Share what you’ve learned

    Maya asks how you handled your first client kickoff.

    “Here’s what happened when I did mine, and what I’d do differently. Take what’s useful.”

  • Week 3: Start with clear direction

    Maya is leading a kickoff for the first time next week.

    “Let’s walk through what good looks like. After the first one, I’ll ask more and tell less.”

Three things to watch

Ask one question, then wait

Silence often means they’re thinking. Before you move on, ask “What else?” The first answer is rarely the whole story.

Ask for their idea before you share yours

Then offer yours as one option among theirs. If the decision is theirs, share your view last.

The approach fits the moment, not the person

Someone who needs direction on new work this week may need room to think next week.

If someone brings up something personal or health-related, listen, and point them to support such as an employee assistance program or HR. You don’t need to solve it.

About this guide

This guide is a one-page picture of the idea behind the Coaching Approach Picker. Choosing between coaching, sharing your experience, and giving direction is adapted from infographics by Amy Gibson. The four parts of a coaching conversation draw on the GROW™ model, developed by Sir John Whitmore and colleagues. Build on what’s working draws on the solution-focused approach of Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg, and colleagues. Weigh the options draws on Irving Janis and Leon Mann’s decisional balance sheet. Turn it into action draws on Peter Hawkins’s CLEAR model. Reconnect with the work draws on Max Landsberg’s Skill/Will Matrix. The asking-before-telling idea draws on Edgar Schein’s Humble Inquiry, and the “What else?” tip comes from Michael Bungay Stanier’s The Coaching Habit®. The approach names, the drawing, and the wording are Pivot Labs’ own.
