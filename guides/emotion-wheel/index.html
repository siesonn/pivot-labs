Emotion Wheel

Someone asks how the meeting went, and all you’ve got is “stressed” or “fine.” This guide gives you six core feelings, with six more exact words under each, so you can say what’s going on. A second picture shows what each feeling often points to and one next move. It’s for reflection, not a diagnosis.

Find your word

Start with the core feeling that’s closest, then look under it for the word that fits best. Good feelings count too. You don’t need the perfect word. A closer one is enough to start.

What it might be telling you

A feeling often points to something you need or care about. Use these as things to consider, then choose one next move.

If a feeling stays heavy, talk with someone you trust or a professional.

An example

Example: a deadline moves up

Your director moves a report deadline up by a week. A coworker asks how you’re doing and you say, “Stressed.”

Find the word

On the wheel, “stressed” could sit under Fear. The closest word might be Overwhelmed.

See what it points to

Fear often points to uncertainty, or to something you care about feeling at risk. Here, you care about handing in good work and you’re not sure it fits in the new time.

Choose a next move

Name the one thing you don’t know, then ask for it. For example, “If this date is firm, what can come off my list?”

A different word would lead somewhere else. If “Frustrated” fit better, that’s Anger, which often points to a standard or a need that wasn’t respected. The next move there starts with a pause.

The Emotion Wheel walks you through this in four short steps: name the feeling, rate how strong it is, think about what might be behind it, and choose what could help. It ends with a one-page check-in note you can print. Your entries clear when you close the page.

About this guide

This guide follows the idea behind feeling wheels: a few basic feelings, with more exact words under each. Well-known versions include Robert Plutchik’s wheel of emotions (1980), Gloria Willcox’s Feeling Wheel (1982), and the Junto Institute’s Emotion & Feeling Wheel. Research on how people group emotion words, such as the work of Shaver and colleagues (1987), points to a similar shape. The wheel, the word list, and the wording are Pivot Labs’ own. This is a reflection tool, not therapy or a diagnosis.
