Visual guide · Johari Window

What others see, and what you share

You think you’re known for being organized. Your team thinks you’re the calm one when things go sideways. Both may be true, and nobody has said either out loud. The Johari Window is a simple way to compare how you see yourself with how others see you. Everyone uses the same list of words, then you talk about what you find.

One list, two views

1

You pick five or six words that describe you at work.

2

A few people you trust each pick five or six words for you, from the same list, on their own.

3

Every word on the list is a strength, so there are no wrong picks.

Four boxes

Where each word lands depends on who picked it.

Known to you
Not known to you
Known to others
Not known to others

  • Open

    Known to you and to others

    You and others picked these.

    It’s what people already see in you.

  • Blind spot

    Known to others, not to you

    Others picked these. You didn’t.

    It’s a strength people notice that you may not count.

  • Hidden

    Known to you, not to others

    You picked these. Others didn’t, yet.

    It’s a strength you know about that people haven’t seen much.

  • Unknown

    Not picked by anyone

    No one picked these this time.

    That doesn’t mean they’re missing.

Two ways to grow the Open box

Ask

Ask people what they see. Your Blind spot gets smaller.

“When have you seen me be calm?”

Share

Tell people something about how you work. Your Hidden box gets smaller.

“I like digging into the numbers. Bring me that kind of work.”

Running it with a team

  1. 1

    Everyone picks their own words first, without looking at anyone else’s.

  2. 2

    Each person picks words for a few teammates, on their own.

  3. 3

    Enter the picks on one screen, where each set hides once it’s added, or print the word list and pick on paper.

  4. 4

    Each person looks at their window, then talks through one box with the group or a partner.

An example

Example: Sam’s window

The picks

Sam picks organized, dependable, thorough, analytical and prepared.

Three teammates pick words for Sam. Two of them choose calm, and one chooses steady and approachable.

The window

Openorganized, dependable
Blind spotcalm, steady, approachable
Hiddenthorough, analytical, prepared
Unknownthe rest of the list

The talk

Sam asks, “When have you seen me be calm?” A teammate names the week a big launch slipped.

Sam shares that the prep work is the part Sam enjoys most, and offers to help others get ready for big meetings.

Three things to watch

It’s a conversation, not a rating

Don’t use it for reviews, ratings or hiring decisions.

Pick on your own, then talk

Seeing other people’s picks first changes your own.

An empty box isn’t a gap

An empty Open box is common, and it’s a good place to start talking.

The Johari Window tool has the full list of 48 words. You pick yours, add what others picked, and see your window with questions to talk about. You can print the word list for a team. Nothing is saved, so print or save your window before you close the page.

About this guide

This guide is a one-page picture of the idea behind the Johari Window tool. The Johari Window was created by Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham in 1955; the name joins their first names, Joe and Harry. Luft described the Open box growing in two ways: when you share more about yourself, and when you ask others what they see. The word list, the drawing, the example and the wording are Pivot Labs’ own.

Sources: Luft, J., & Ingham, H. (1955). The Johari window, a graphic model of interpersonal awareness. Proceedings of the Western Training Laboratory in Group Development. University of California, Los Angeles. · Luft, J. (1969). Of human interaction. National Press Books.
