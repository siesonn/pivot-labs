Six ways to start the conversation

You need to talk with someone about their work. Maybe it’s a thank-you that’s overdue, or something that keeps happening. Either way, the hardest part is often the first few sentences. This guide helps you choose how to start, based on what the feedback is about and what’s already been said.

Three questions

Answer them in order. Together they point to the approach that fits.

1

What’s it about?

Something that went well, something to grow toward, or something that needs to change.

2

Did you see or hear it yourself?

If someone else told you, start by getting their view.

3

Has it come up before?

The more times you’ve raised it, the more direct the conversation gets.

Six ways to start

When it’s going well

  • Recognize what’s working

    Name what they did and the difference it made, so they know what to keep doing.

    Best when they did something you want to see more of.

  • Look ahead

    Agree on one thing to work toward, and trade ideas for the next chance to try it.

    Best for growth, when nothing needs fixing.

When something needs to change

From gentlest to most direct.

GentlestMost direct

  • Get their view first

    Share what you’ve heard in neutral terms, ask what happened, and listen before you decide on any feedback.

    Best when you heard about it but didn’t see it yourself.

  • Describe and agree

    Describe what happened and its effect, hear their view, and agree on one next step.

    Best for the first conversation about something to change.

  • Name the pattern

    Link this moment to your earlier conversation, ask what’s getting in the way, and solve it together.

    Best when you’ve raised it once and it has happened again.

  • Set a clear expectation

    Review what you’ve discussed, then say what needs to change, by when, and what happens if it continues.

    Best when you’ve raised it more than once and it’s still happening.

An example

Example: Jordan’s client calls

Jordan often answers before clients finish their questions. Here’s how the same issue might move through three conversations.

  • The first time: Describe and agree

    “In Tuesday’s call, you answered before the client finished their question, twice. They had to repeat it, and the call ran 15 minutes over. What was going on from your side?”

    Then agree on one next step.

  • It happens again: Name the pattern

    “After the March 3 call, we agreed you’d let clients finish before answering. It happened again on Tuesday. What’s getting in the way?”

  • It’s still happening: Set a clear expectation

    “We’ve talked about this twice. By the end of the month, I need clients to finish their questions before you answer. I’ll join two calls a week and give you notes right after. If it continues, we’ll need to talk about what happens next.”

And when Jordan gets it right, that’s its own conversation: “In today’s call, you let the client finish, then answered both questions. They thanked you for listening.”

Three things to watch

Describe actions, not traits

“Careless” or “rude” describes a person. “You answered before the client finished” describes what happened, and gives them something to change. Leave out guesses about why until you’ve asked.

Keep what’s working separate

If something’s going well, say so on its own, so it doesn’t sound like a cushion for the hard part.

Check your part first

Think about unclear expectations, workload, or missing tools. Would you say this the same way to anyone on the team? Wait until you’re calm enough to listen.

About this guide

This guide is a one-page picture of the idea behind the Feedback Conversation Planner. Describing when and where something happened, what the person did, and its effect comes from the Situation-Behavior-Impact™ (SBI™) model by the Center for Creative Leadership. Opening with context and closing with an agreed next step come from Anna Carroll’s COIN conversation model. Look ahead draws on feedforward, Marshall Goldsmith’s term for offering ideas for the future instead of reviewing the past. The two approaches for a repeated issue are inspired by Jonathan Raymond’s Accountability Dial®, which raises an issue in gradual steps. The reminder to describe actions, not guesses about why, draws on Chris Argyris’s ladder of inference. The things to watch draw on the planner’s “Before you talk” checklist, adapted from infographics by Amy Gibson and Cicely Simpson. The approach names, the drawing, and the wording are Pivot Labs’ own.

SBI and Situation-Behavior-Impact are trademarks of the Center for Creative Leadership.
