Skill and will

Skill is the knowledge and experience to do a task well. Will is the motivation and confidence to take it on right now. Two questions about one task point to one of four ways to support someone.

Two questions

Ask them about one task, one at a time, because the answers can differ.

How ready are they to do this task well?

Think about work you have seen them do, not their title, how long they have been here, or how confident they sound.

How willing are they to take it on right now?

Think about this task and this moment. Willingness can shift with workload, interest, and confidence.

Skill and will are separate. Someone can be very skilled and not want the work, or want it a lot and still be learning.

Four ways to support

Put the two answers together and the task lands in one of four boxes. Each box suggests a way to help. Move a task when things change.

  • Guide

    Willing, still building skill

    Show how it is done, let them try a piece, and give feedback soon after.

    Example: a teammate who asked to run the weekly meeting for the first time.

  • Stretch

    Skilled and willing

    Hand over ownership. Agree on the outcome and the check-ins, then step back.

    Example: a teammate who leads meetings well and wants to lead the quarterly review.

  • Direct

    Building skill, hesitant

    Give clear steps and a short first goal. Check in often.

    Example: a new hire asked to build a report in a tool they have not used.

  • Re-engage

    Skilled, hesitant

    Find out what is in the way before changing anything.

    Example: an experienced teammate who has gone quiet on a report they used to own.

A third question: what is behind the hesitation?

Direct and Re-engage both start with hesitation, and the cause changes the first step. Choose the closest fit.

A full workload or competing priorities

Ask what would need to move to make room, and agree on priorities together.

Not sure they can do it well

Start with a smaller piece, point to related work they have done well, and stay available for questions.

Unclear why it matters or what is expected

Explain why the task matters and what a good result looks like.

Not interested in this kind of work

Ask what would make the work worth their effort, or whether someone else is a better fit.

If you do not know, ask what is getting in the way, and listen before suggesting changes.

Three things to watch

One task, not the whole person

A placement describes this task at this moment. The same person can be a Stretch on one task and a Guide on another.

Willingness can change

Workload, interest, and how the task is set up can all shift it. Check again when things change.

Not sure? Find out first

If you have not seen them do the work, watch a small part of it or ask how they would approach it. If you have not asked how they feel about it, ask.

About this guide

This guide is a one-page picture of the idea behind the Team Development Map. The idea of matching how you support someone to their skill and will for a specific task comes from the Skill/Will Matrix that Max Landsberg describes in The Tao of Coaching (1996). Pivot Labs renamed two of his four approaches, Delegate and Excite, as Stretch and Re-engage, and added the question about what might be behind hesitation. The drawing and wording are Pivot Labs’ own.

Source: Landsberg, M. (1996). The Tao of Coaching. HarperCollins.
