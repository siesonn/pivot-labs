Who decides what

You handed something off, and the questions still come back to you. Or you find out later about a call you’d have wanted a say in. Usually nobody agreed on who decides what. This guide gives you three levels to sort that out before the work starts.

Three levels

For each decision that might come up, pick one level. Agree on it together, so you both know where the line is.

More up to themMore with you

  • They decide

    They make the call and keep going. Their way may be different from yours, and that can still be a good way. It comes up at your regular check-in if it matters.

  • They decide, then tell you

    They make the call, then let you know soon after, so you’re not caught off guard.

  • Check with you first

    They come to you before acting. It helps if they bring what they’d suggest.

A starting point

These are the choices the Delegation Planner starts with. They’re the same for everyone, so change any that don’t fit the work.

  • They decide

    • How to do the work: methods, tools, and order of steps
    • Day-to-day priorities within the work

  • They decide, then tell you

    • Asking others for help
    • Updates to senior leaders, clients, or other teams

  • Check with you first

    • Spending money
    • Changing the deadline
    • Changing what done looks like

If you’re comfortable with some spending, set an amount. Up to that amount, they decide. Over it, they check with you. Leave out anything that won’t come up, and add anything that’s missing.

An example

Example: handing off the monthly vendor report

You’re handing the monthly vendor report to Jordan. Jordan has done parts of it before, with help.

  • They decide

    Which vendors to follow up with first, how to lay out the report, and what to work on each day.

  • They decide, then tell you

    Asking finance for a missing number, and sending the finished report to the team.

  • Check with you first

    Moving the due date past the 5th, dropping the late-vendor flags, or spending any money.

After a few months of reports going well, you might move spending up to $500 to “They decide, then tell you.” Start where you’re both comfortable, then loosen.

Three things to watch

The level fits the decision, not the person

It says how this call gets made right now. It isn’t a verdict on how capable someone is.

Loosen it as it goes well

Starting with more “check with you first” is fine. Look again at your check-ins and move a decision up a level when it makes sense.

If decisions keep coming back to you

Maybe the line isn’t clear yet, or they don’t have the access or authority to act. Ask what’s getting in the way before you step back in.

About this guide

This guide is a one-page picture of one step in the Delegation Planner. Its three levels are a simpler version of the five degrees of initiative that William Oncken, Jr. and Donald L. Wass describe in “Management Time: Who’s Got the Monkey?” They write that every handoff needs an agreed level of initiative and a set time for the next check-in. The suggested starting points, the drawing, and the wording are Pivot Labs’ own.

Source: Oncken, W., Jr., & Wass, D. L. (1974). Management time: Who’s got the monkey? Harvard Business Review.
